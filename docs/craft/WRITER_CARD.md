# Writer card (read by the writer model every episode)

Goal: a ~55-second sketch that ordinary Indian viewers laugh at out loud, understand on first view, and send to a
specific person. Evidence: docs/research/R1–R5. [F] research · [E] expert · [H] our hypothesis.

## §1 Observe before you invent
- Start from a TRUE, SPECIFIC behaviour, not a topic. Name the object, phrase, number or ritual ("keeps the sweets box
  for the next guest", not "Diwali gifts"). The best comedy states a bitter truth people recognise [E Helitzer; Gullak/TVF
  writers work from lived experience].
- Write down the unspoken rule everyone follows. The comedy lives in breaking or exposing it.
- LLM ideas converge on the same tropes [F Jentzsch & Kersting; Wenger & Kenett]. So: mine many observations at high
  variety, skip anything on the cliché list unless your angle is genuinely new, and never repeat an earlier episode.

## §2 Premise card (no jokes yet — story first [E Ganz, Jamin])
- **Violation + benign** [F McGraw & Warren]: name the norm/expectation/status that is broken AND why it stays harmless
  (a competing norm, low stakes, or distance). Small mishaps work with close, familiar characters; anything serious must be
  hypothetical or cartoon-distant [F McGraw et al. 2012]. No real tragedy.
- **Wants**: every on-screen person wants something concrete right now; someone blocks it; why now? [E Mamet, Sorkin]
- **Game**: one pattern that repeats and heightens about 3 times, each time a different lever (stakes, specifics,
  emotion). "If this is true, what else is true?" [E UCB]
- **Status**: someone's status rises or falls; the payoff is a flip or a collapse [E Johnstone].
- **First-time viewer**: who these people are to each other must be clear in the first ~5 s through how they address
  each other and where they are — never exposition [F Quaglio: sitcom dialogue uses more vocatives].
- **Showable**: the funniest moments must be visible with our puppets; any person physically present is on screen.
- **Share trigger**: who forwards it to whom, and why ("this is literally my mother") [E Berger STEPPS]. Aim for
  energetic amusement and surprise; avoid sad or bitter endings [F Berger & Milkman; Teixeira et al.].
- **Fresh angle**: one sentence on why no meme page has done this exact take.

## §3 Shape of a ~55-second sketch (beat labels used in scripts and shots)
| beat | time | job |
|---|---|---|
| hook | 0–3 s | conflict or a strange specific in the first line (≤9 words); a surprise early holds attention [F Teixeira] |
| setup | 3–10 s | base reality + who-is-who cue + the first unusual thing |
| escalate1/2/3 | 10–42 s | the game three times, each bigger, a different lever; a laugh or smile at least every ~8–10 s (bursts of joy beat one late payoff [F Teixeira]) |
| turn | 42–48 s | reversal or reframe: the setup turns out to fit a second meaning [F script opposition] |
| punch | 48–53 s | the biggest laugh; stillness and a short pause before it [F Ma et al., correlational] |
| button | 53–58 s | one short extra laugh that also leads back into the first line (loop) |
110–160 spoken words, 14–22 lines. Never pad; never cut a line's audio to fit — cut a weaker line instead.

## §4 Building a joke
- **Two meanings**: the setup must fit meaning A; the reveal forces meaning B and makes sense afterwards. Random endings
  fail [F Israel et al.; Raskin]. Write A and B for every joke in the joke map.
- **Reveal word last**: the word that flips A→B ends the line; nothing explains it afterwards. Hindi is verb-final —
  restructure, or put the reveal in a short tag line.
- **Pattern, pattern, shift**: two similar beats then a contrasting third [F Loewenstein & Heath].
- **Callbacks**: plant a distinctive word or object early (first 10–20 s) and bring it back with a new meaning at the
  end; never show the punch word right before its punch [F Topolinski].
- **Target check**: laugh at habits, situations and the powerful, never at caste, religion, region, body, disability or
  gender as a group [F Kant & Norman; YouTube policy; BNS s.299].
- **Unfunny test**: for each joke line, imagine the plain unfunny version. If it barely differs, it isn't a joke yet
  (models are good at removing humour [F Horvitz et al.] — use that to test, not to write).

## §5 Dialogue
- Every line comes from a want. Characters dodge and complain about something nearby instead of naming feelings
  (subtext); nobody explains the joke or says "as you know" [E Mamet].
- **Complete, natural sentences** a real person from UP/Delhi says — not fragments, not stage Hindi, not stock
  phrases ("kya baat hai", "kitne sweet"). Read it aloud: would your cousin say exactly this?
- Real talk: quick replies (~0.2 s gaps), the odd interruption or overlap, one "matlab/haan toh" or self-correction per
  character — flavour, not stumbling [F Levinson & Torreira; Quaglio]. No tidy line-for-line ping-pong.
- English for office/tech/status words, Hindi for feelings, scolding and punchlines [H; code-mixing effect is tiny].
- Each character sounds like their bible entry (catch rhythm, vocabulary). One specific detail per line beats adjectives.
- 3–10 words per line (≤14). Captions: ≤2 lines, ≤42 characters each; break so the reveal word ends the caption.

## §6 Punch-up (after the judge's notes)
- Keep the story, premise and cast. For each weak line, write 3 alternatives and keep the funniest complete one [E alts].
- Cut every line that neither gets a laugh nor moves the story. Shorter is funnier when in doubt.
- Re-check: hook ≤9 words; who-is-who clear by 5 s; reveal words last; callback planted; loop bridge works.

## §7 Never
Premises about nothing; random events posing as jokes; catchphrases forced in; a moral at the end; jokes that need the
caption to explain them; the same situation or joke engine as a recent episode; real named people or brands mocked.
